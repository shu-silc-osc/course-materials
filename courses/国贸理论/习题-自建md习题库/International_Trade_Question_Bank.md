All questions, directions, and answers in this file are in English. For analytical questions, show the economic mechanism as well as the conclusion.

## Chapter 2: Gravity and Trade Patterns

#### Multiple-Choice Questions

1. In the basic gravity model, bilateral trade tends to be larger when:
   A. both economies are smaller and farther apart  
   B. either economy is larger and the countries are closer  
   C. both countries impose higher tariffs  
   D. the countries have fewer transport links

> Difficulty: Basic

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

B.  GDP raises trade capacity and income; distance raises trade costs.

</details>

2. A country trades less than its GDP and distance predict. Which factor could explain the deviation?
   A. Shared language  B. A trade agreement  C. A border delay  D. A larger port network

> Difficulty: Intermediate

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

C.  Border formalities and delays can reduce trade; the other choices generally facilitate it.

#### Short Answer

</details>

3. Why can a border reduce trade even when two countries share a language and have a trade agreement?

> Difficulty: Basic

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Customs procedures, time costs, currency conversion, and remaining tariffs or regulatory differences still raise the cost of cross-border exchange.

## Chapter 3: Comparative Advantage

#### Calculation and Application

</details>

4. Home needs 2 labour hours per unit of cloth and 4 hours per unit of food. Foreign needs 6 hours per unit of cloth and 3 hours per unit of food. Find each country’s opportunity cost of cloth and identify comparative advantage.

> Difficulty: Intermediate

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Home’s opportunity cost is 2/4 = 0.5 units of food per cloth. Foreign’s is 6/3 = 2 units of food per cloth. Home has comparative advantage in cloth; Foreign has comparative advantage in food.

</details>

5. Why can a country with an absolute advantage in both goods still gain from trade?

> Difficulty: Intermediate

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Absolute advantage does not determine relative opportunity costs. Specialising in the good with lower opportunity cost allows resources to be used more efficiently and expands consumption possibilities.

## Chapter 4: Specific Factors

</details>

6. When the relative price of a country’s export good rises, identify the likely short-run effects on labour allocation and the returns to specific factors.

> Difficulty: Intermediate

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Labour moves toward the export industry. The specific factor in that industry gains; the specific factor in the import-competing industry loses. Workers’ real-wage effects depend on the goods they consume.

## Chapter 5: Heckscher–Ohlin Theory

</details>

7. State the Heckscher–Ohlin theorem and distinguish it from the Stolper–Samuelson theorem.

> Difficulty: Basic

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Heckscher–Ohlin predicts that a country exports the good that uses its abundant factor intensively. Stolper–Samuelson predicts how a change in a good’s relative price affects the real returns to factors.

</details>

8. What is the Leontief paradox?

> Difficulty: Basic

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The finding that U.S. exports appeared less capital-intensive than U.S. import substitutes, contrary to the simple prediction that a capital-abundant country exports capital-intensive goods.

## Chapter 6: Standard Trade Model

</details>

9. Define the terms of trade and explain one channel through which a large-country import tariff may affect welfare.

> Difficulty: Basic

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The terms of trade are the price of exports relative to imports. A large-country tariff may lower the foreign export price and improve the home terms of trade, but its production and consumption distortions may outweigh that gain.

</details>

10. Distinguish the production point from the consumption point in a trade diagram.

> Difficulty: Basic

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The production point is where the relative-price line is tangent to the PPF; the consumption point is selected on the feasible trade-budget line according to preferences. They differ when the country trades.

## Chapter 7: External Economies

</details>

11. Give three sources of external economies of scale.

> Difficulty: Basic

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Specialised suppliers, pooled labour markets, and knowledge spillovers.

</details>

12. Explain path dependence in the international location of production.

> Difficulty: Basic

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

An early location advantage can attract firms and suppliers, lowering costs further. The resulting cluster may persist even if another location later has better underlying resources.

## Chapter 8: Firms and Trade

</details>

13. Why do only some firms export when trade barriers fall?

> Difficulty: Basic

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Firms differ in productivity and exporting has fixed costs. Only firms productive enough to cover those costs enter export markets.

</details>

14. What is intra-industry trade?

> Difficulty: Basic

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Two-way trade in differentiated products within the same industry, supported by economies of scale and consumers’ preference for variety.

## Chapter 9: Trade Policy

</details>

15. Compare a small-country tariff with a large-country tariff.

> Difficulty: Intermediate

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

A small country takes the world price as given, so the tariff creates domestic distortions without a terms-of-trade gain. A large country may lower the foreign export price and improve its terms of trade, although welfare still depends on whether that gain exceeds the distortion cost.

</details>

16. Who normally receives quota rents under an import quota and a voluntary export restraint, respectively?

> Difficulty: Basic

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Under an import quota, rents go to holders of import licences; under a voluntary export restraint, rents generally accrue to foreign exporters.

## Comprehensive Questions

</details>

17. A country is labour-abundant and cloth is labour-intensive. Predict the trade pattern and identify a group that could lose from the resulting price change.

> Difficulty: Intermediate

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The country tends to export cloth. If trade lowers the domestic relative price of food or raises the relative price of cloth, factor returns change according to the relevant factor-intensity mechanism; owners of a factor used intensively in import-competing production may lose. State the price change before naming winners and losers.

</details>

18. A government proposes subsidising a new export industry because it may learn and lower costs over time. Give the economic rationale and two policy risks.

> Difficulty: Basic

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Dynamic economies and learning by doing may create future cost reductions that private firms cannot fully capture. Risks include government inability to identify a viable industry, lobbying or capture, fiscal cost, and retaliation. The expected external benefit must exceed these costs.

</details>

19. A government claims an import surge proves dumping and asks for an anti-dumping duty. What additional evidence is needed?

> Difficulty: Basic

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Evidence of dumping under the applicable comparison, material injury or threat to the domestic industry, and a causal link, established through the required investigation and procedure.

</details>

20. A firm moves customer service operations abroad but continues to sell the same product domestically. Explain why this is service outsourcing and identify one reason the activity may remain local.

> Difficulty: Intermediate

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The firm has relocated a service operation across borders, which is service outsourcing/offshoring. The service may need face-to-face contact or proximity to customers, making remote provision impractical.

</details>
