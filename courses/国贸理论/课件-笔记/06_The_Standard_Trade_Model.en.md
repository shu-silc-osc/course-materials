# Chapter 6: The Standard Trade Model

> [!abstract] Learning objectives
> Use relative supply and demand, the terms of trade, and social indifference curves to analyse growth, trade policy, and international borrowing and lending.

## Framework and terms of trade

The standard trade model includes the Ricardian, specific-factors, and Heckscher–Ohlin models as special cases. It considers food (F) and cloth (C), with a smooth PPF in each country. Differences in factors, technology, and resources create different frontiers. A country’s PPF determines relative supply; preferences determine relative demand; their intersection determines the world relative price. The terms of trade are an export-price index divided by an import-price index. All else equal, an improvement usually raises purchasing power and welfare.

The equilibrium determines production and consumption together. The relative-price line is tangent to the PPF at the production point; a social indifference curve determines the consumption point; trade balances the budget between them. Figures 6-1 and 6-2 show how prices affect production and relative supply. Figures 6-3 and 6-4 combine production, consumption, and trade. Figures 6-5a/b show world relative-price equilibria.

## Growth, tariffs, and subsidies

Growth biased toward export production shifts relative supply outward and may lower the export good’s relative price. Growth biased toward import substitutes can improve the terms of trade. A large country may affect world prices; a small country generally takes them as given. Immiserizing growth requires a strong export bias and a sufficiently adverse terms-of-trade effect.

An import tariff reduces imports and changes domestic relative prices, production, and consumption. A large country may lower the foreign export price and improve its terms of trade, but the tariff also distorts production and consumption. National welfare rises only if the terms-of-trade gain exceeds the distortion cost. An export subsidy supports exporters but worsens the home terms of trade and creates a fiscal cost. Figures 6-6–6-10 illustrate biased growth, world supply and demand, a food tariff, and a cloth export subsidy.

## International borrowing and lending

Intertemporal trade exchanges current resources for future resources. A borrower consumes more than current output and repays later; a lender gives up current resources for a future return. Treat current and future consumption as two goods, with the interest rate determining their relative price.

## Check yourself

- The terms of trade are a price ratio, not the trade balance.
- Keep the production point separate from the consumption point; trade can make them differ.
- Tariffs and subsidies work through both terms-of-trade and domestic-distortion channels.

The Welfare Effects of Changes in the 
Terms of Trade
• The terms of trade refers to the price of exports 
relative to the price of imports.
– When a country exports cloth and the relative 
price of cloth increases, the terms of trade rise.
• Because a higher relative price for exports means 
that the country can afford to buy more imports, an 
increase in the terms of trade increases a country’s 
welfare.
• A decline in the terms of trade decreases a 
country’s welfare.

### Slide 31

1-31 
Relative Price and Supply Effects of a 
Tariff
• If the home country imposes a tariff on food 
imports, the price of food relative to the price of 
cloth rises for domestic consumers.
– Likewise, the price of cloth relative to the price of food falls 
for domestic consumers.
– Domestic producers will receive a lower relative price of 
cloth, and therefore will be more willing to switch to food 
production: relative supply of cloth will decrease.
– Domestic consumers will pay a lower relative price for cloth, 
and therefore will be more willing to switch to cloth 
consumption: relative demand for cloth will increase.
