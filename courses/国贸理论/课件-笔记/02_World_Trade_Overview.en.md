# Chapter 2: World Trade — An Overview

> [!abstract] Learning objectives
> Use the gravity model to explain bilateral trade, identify the roles of distance, borders, culture, geography, and trade agreements, and describe changes in world trade and service outsourcing.

## 1. The gravity model

Bilateral trade tends to increase with the size of either economy and decline with distance:

$$T_{ij}=A\frac{Y_iY_j}{D_{ij}}$$

$T_{ij}$ is trade between countries $i$ and $j$; $Y_i$ and $Y_j$ are their GDPs; $D_{ij}$ is distance; and $A$ is a constant. A more flexible version is $T_{ij}=A Y_i^aY_j^b/D_{ij}^c$. Estimates cited in the lecture associate a 1% increase in distance with roughly a 0.7%–1% decrease in trade.

Large economies produce and sell more, while their higher incomes support more imports. The U.S.–Europe examples show that Germany, the United Kingdom, and France were among the United States’ largest European trading partners, consistent with their large GDPs. The related scatterplot places economic size against trade with the United States and shows a positive association.

## 2. Additional determinants of trade

- **Cultural affinity:** shared language and migration history can strengthen trade. Ireland’s trade with the United States exceeds a simple gravity prediction.
- **Geography:** harbours and fewer natural barriers make transport easier. Location helps explain the Netherlands’ and Belgium’s stronger-than-predicted trade.
- **Trade agreements:** agreements reduce tariffs and border formalities. The lecture uses the 1994 NAFTA agreement among the United States, Canada, and Mexico as an example.
- **Borders:** paperwork, delays, currency conversion, and tariffs raise trade costs. A U.S.–Canada agreement and shared language do not eliminate the border effect. Comparisons of trade between Canadian provinces and between Canadian provinces and U.S. states show lower cross-border trade even after accounting for distance.
- **Multinational firms:** divisions in different countries import and export goods between themselves.

The British Columbia map and trade table compare provincial and U.S.-state links, including trade as a share of GDP. Interpret them by separating distance from border costs; proximity alone does not imply frictionless trade.

## 3. Globalization and the changing composition of trade

Transport and communication innovations—from wheels, sails, railways, and steam power to aircraft, computers, the internet, fibre optics, and GPS—have reduced the practical effect of distance. Wars and economic crises can nevertheless change trade patterns more sharply than technology does.

World trade grew rapidly from 1870 to 1913, fell during the two world wars and the Great Depression, began recovering around 1945, and did not fully recover until around 1970. Since then, trade as a share of world GDP has reached unprecedented levels. The historical line chart should be read as expansion, collapse, post-war recovery, and renewed growth.

For 2011, the lecture reports approximate world-trade shares of 53% manufactured products, 20% services, 19% minerals, and 8% agricultural products. Historical comparisons show a shift toward manufactured goods: Britain and the United States traded more agricultural and mineral products in 1910, while manufactured products dominated their imports and exports by 2002. Low- and middle-income economies also shifted exports: manufactured goods rose from about 12% in 1960 to 65% in 2001, while agricultural products fell from about 58% to 10%. The charts show this change over time by product category.

## 4. Service outsourcing

Service outsourcing, or offshoring, occurs when a firm moves service operations abroad. It is most feasible when a service can be transmitted electronically, such as a call centre. Many services must be delivered near the customer. About 60% of jobs are described as non-tradable, so the share of employment in potentially tradable industries is not the same as the volume of services actually traded.

### Slide 8

1-8 
 The Gravity Model 
• The gravity model assumes that size and distance 
are important for trade in the following way:
Tij = A x Yi x Yj /Dij
where 
Tij is the value of trade between country i and country j
A is a constant
Yi the GDP of country I, Yj is the GDP of country j
Dij is the distance between country i and country j
• Or more generally
Tij = A x Yia x Yjb /Dijc
where a, b, and c are allowed to differ from 1.
