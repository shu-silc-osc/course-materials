# Chapter 4: Specific Factors and Income Distribution

> [!abstract] Learning objectives
> Use the specific-factors model to explain how trade can raise aggregate welfare while changing income across industries, workers, and owners of capital or land.

## Model structure

The economy produces manufactures and food. Labour can move between industries, while capital or land is specific to one industry in the short run. Production functions have diminishing marginal returns to labour. Workers move toward the industry with the higher value of marginal product until the two industries’ labour demands imply a common wage. Diminishing returns make the production frontier bowed outward.

## Relative-price changes and income distribution

A change in relative goods prices shifts labour demand and output across industries. When the relative price of an export good rises, labour demand in that industry increases and workers move into it. The specific factor in the export industry gains; the specific factor in the import-competing industry loses. Workers’ real-wage effects depend on their consumption basket: a higher nominal wage does not guarantee greater purchasing power for every good. Trade can expand aggregate consumption possibilities without benefiting every group.

The production-function and marginal-product diagrams show diminishing labour productivity when industry-specific capital is fixed. The labour-allocation diagram uses the intersection of value marginal products to determine wages and employment. The trade diagrams connect relative-price changes to output, relative supply, and trade direction. The budget-line diagram shows that trade allows consumption beyond the domestic production frontier.

## International labour mobility

Migration changes labour supply in both destination and origin economies, affecting wages, employment, and returns to specific factors. Evaluate migration by group and over time rather than by aggregate output alone; adjustment speed and long-run adaptation matter.

### Slide 16

•1-16 
Production Possibilities (cont.)
• Opportunity cost of producing one more 
yard of cloth is MPLF/MPLC pounds of food.
– To produce one more yard of cloth, you need 
1/MPLC hours of labor.
– To free up one hour of labor, you must reduce 
output of food by MPLF pounds.
– To produce less food and more cloth, employ less 
in food and more in cloth.
• The marginal product of labor in food rises and the 
marginal product of labor in cloth falls, so MPLF/MPLC 
rises.
