# Chapter 3: Labor Productivity and Comparative Advantage — The Ricardian Model

> [!abstract] Learning objectives
> Determine comparative advantage from opportunity cost; build the one-factor Ricardian model; derive specialization, trade prices, relative wages, and gains from trade; and recognize the model’s limits and empirical support.

## 1. Absolute advantage and comparative advantage

Absolute advantage compares output per unit of input. Comparative advantage compares the opportunity cost of production. A country may be more efficient in every good yet have comparative advantage in only the good with the lower relative opportunity cost. Trade is based on comparative rather than absolute advantage.

The lecture introduces the intuition with roses and computers, then formalizes it with cheese and wine. Let $a_{LC}$ and $a_{LW}$ denote the home labor hours required per unit of cheese and wine; starred values denote foreign requirements. The opportunity cost of cheese is $a_{LC}/a_{LW}$ units of wine.

## 2. The one-factor model and production possibilities

Assumptions: labour is the only input; labour productivity differs across countries because of technology but is constant within each country; labour supply is fixed; there are two countries and two goods; and perfect competition pays workers the value of their output and lets them move to the higher-wage industry.

Home’s labour constraint and production possibility frontier (PPF) are:

$$a_{LC}Q_C+a_{LW}Q_W\leq L,\qquad Q_W=\frac{L}{a_{LW}}-\frac{a_{LC}}{a_{LW}}Q_C$$

Maximum cheese output is $L/a_{LC}$; maximum wine output is $L/a_{LW}$. The absolute slope of the PPF equals the opportunity cost of cheese. With $L=1{,}000$, $a_{LC}=1$, and $a_{LW}=2$, the constraint is $Q_C+2Q_W\leq1{,}000$, giving intercepts of 1,000 pounds of cheese and 500 gallons of wine. Figure 3-1’s intercepts represent complete specialization; its slope represents constant opportunity cost.

## 3. Relative prices, wages, and specialization

Hourly wages are $P_C/a_{LC}$ in cheese and $P_W/a_{LW}$ in wine. Workers choose the higher-paying industry:

- If $P_C/P_W>a_{LC}/a_{LW}$, home specializes in cheese.
- If $P_C/P_W<a_{LC}/a_{LW}$, home specializes in wine.
- If the two ratios are equal, workers are indifferent and both goods may be produced. Without trade, producing both requires the relative price to equal opportunity cost.

The foreign PPF slope reflects foreign opportunity cost. Figure 3-2 uses a steeper foreign frontier to show that foreign gives up more wine to produce cheese.

## 4. Trade equilibrium and gains

Suppose home has comparative advantage in cheese: $a_{LC}/a_{LW}<a^*_{LC}/a^*_{LW}$. Without trade, cheese has a higher relative price abroad, so home exports cheese and foreign exports wine. World relative supply is $(Q_C+Q_C^*)/(Q_W+Q_W^*)$; relative demand for cheese falls as its relative price rises.

World relative supply is a step function. Below both countries’ opportunity costs, both produce wine. At home’s opportunity cost, home workers may switch between goods. Between the two opportunity costs, home specializes in cheese and foreign in wine. At foreign’s opportunity cost, foreign workers may also switch; above both costs, both countries produce cheese. Figure 3-3 shows the step-shaped RS curve and downward-sloping RD curve; their intersection determines the trade price.

Trade shifts resources toward each country’s lower-opportunity-cost good and lets export income purchase imports. Consumption can therefore lie beyond the domestic PPF. Figure 3-4 links the production point, the trade-price line, and the consumption point to show the expanded consumption set.

## 5. Numerical example and relative wages

Home has $a_{LC}=1$ hour per pound of cheese and $a_{LW}=2$ hours per gallon of wine; foreign has $a^*_{LC}=6$ and $a^*_{LW}=3$. Cheese costs 1/2 gallon of wine at home and 2 gallons abroad. Home is more productive in both goods but has comparative advantage only in cheese. A trade price $P_C/P_W=1$ lies between the two opportunity costs.

If both goods sell for $12, home’s cheese wage is $12 per hour and foreign’s wine wage is $4, so the relative wage is 3. It lies between home’s relative productivity advantages: sixfold in cheese and 1.5-fold in wine. High wages can be offset by high productivity, and low productivity by low wages. The lecture’s evidence links wages and labour productivity across countries. South Korean wages rose as productivity increased from about 5% of the U.S. level in 1975 to productivity of roughly half the U.S. level by 2007.

## 6. Many goods, non-tradables, and evidence

With many goods, home produces good $i$ when $wa_{Li}<w^*a^*_{Li}$; equivalently, home’s relative productivity $a^*_{Li}/a_{Li}$ exceeds the relative wage $w/w^*$. In the five-good example, when $w/w^*=3$, home produces apples, bananas, and caviar, while foreign produces dates and enchiladas. Relative wages are determined by relative labour supply and derived labour demand. A higher relative wage makes home goods less competitive and lowers demand for home labour. Figure 3-5 depicts this equilibrium; an industry can relocate abruptly when its cost threshold is crossed.

The simple model predicts complete specialization, but multiple factors, protectionism, and transport costs limit it. Haircuts and car repairs are examples of non-tradable services. Countries spend a substantial share of income on non-tradables.

Evidence supports the link between productivity and export advantage. In 1951, the United States had an absolute advantage in all 26 manufacturing industries compared with Britain, but its export advantage was weaker in industries where relative productivity was lower. Bangladesh’s average labour productivity was about 28% of China’s, yet apparel productivity was about 77% of China’s, giving Bangladesh comparative advantage in clothing. Productivity differences and comparative advantage help explain trade, although actual production is not fully specialized.

Misconceptions about 
Comparative Advantage
1. Free trade is beneficial only if a country is more 
productive than foreign countries.
– But even an unproductive country benefits from free 
trade by avoiding the high costs for goods that it would 
otherwise have to produce domestically.
– High costs derive from inefficient use of resources.
– The benefits of free trade do not depend on absolute 
advantage, rather they depend on comparative 
advantage: specializing in industries that use resources 
most efficiently.

### Slide 58

1-58 
Misconceptions about 
Comparative Advantage (cont.)
2. Free trade with countries that pay low wages hurts 
high wage countries.
– While trade may reduce wages for some workers, thereby 
affecting the distribution of income within a country, 
trade benefits consumers and other workers.
– Consumers benefit because they can purchase goods 
more cheaply.
– Producers/workers benefit by earning a higher income in 
the industries that use resources more efficiently, 
allowing them to earn higher prices and wages.

### Slide 59

1-59 
Misconceptions about 
Comparative Advantage (cont.)
3. Free trade exploits less productive countries 
whose workers make low wages.
– While labor standards in some countries are less than

– Are high wages and safe labor practices alternatives to

– Consumers benefit from free trade by having access to

– Producers/workers benefit from having higher

### Slide 69

1-69 
Transportation Costs and Non-
traded Goods
• The Ricardian model predicts that countries 
completely specialize in production.
• But this rarely happens for three main 
reasons:
1. More than one factor of production reduces the 
tendency of specialization (Econ/Trade Chapters 
4-5).
2. Protectionism (Econ/Trade Chapters 9–12).
3. Transportation costs reduce or prevent trade, 
which may cause each country to produce the 
same good or service.

### Slide 70

• Nontraded goods and services (ex., haircuts 
and auto repairs) exist due to high transport 
costs.
– Countries tend to spend a large fraction of 
national income on nontraded goods and services.
– This fact has implications for the gravity model 
and for models that consider how income 
transfers across countries affect trade.

Empirical Evidence
• Do countries export those goods in which their 
productivity is relatively high?
• The ratio of U.S. to British exports in 1951 
compared to the ratio of U.S. to British labor 
productivity in 26 manufacturing industries 
suggests yes.
• At this time the U.S. had an absolute advantage in 
all 26 industries, yet the ratio of exports was low in 
the least productive sectors of the U.S.

across countries generate comparative 
advantage.
2. A country has a comparative advantage in 
producing a good when its opportunity cost 
of producing that good is lower than in 
other countries.
