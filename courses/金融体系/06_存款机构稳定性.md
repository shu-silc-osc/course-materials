#### Short-Answer Questions

1. Explain how the moral hazard arises from bailing out ‘too big to fail’ institutions.

> Difficulty: Intermediate

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Governments bail out ‘too big to fail’ institutions because if these institutions fail to meet their
extensive networks of obligations, it would degrade the capacity of the financial system to
perform its functions. Bailing out ‘too big to fail’ institutions, however, creates a moral hazard
as these institutions are likely to take on more risk (than otherwise) when losses are shared by
taxpayers (through bailout payments).

</details>

2. Discuss how the FSB responds to the too-big-to-fail problem.

> Difficulty: Intermediate

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The FSB designates certain financial institutions as Systematically Important Financial
Institutions (SIFIs) and require these SIFIs to satisfy stronger-than-normal regulatory and
supervisory measures, including the establishment of effective resolution regimes. These are
intended to ‘make it possible to resolve any financial institution in an orderly manner without
severe systemic disruption or exposing taxpayers to the risk of loss, by protecting the firm’s
functions that are critical to the financial market or the real economy and ensuring that losses
are borne by shareholders and creditors of the failing firm, as they would be in insolvency’
(FSB, 2018). Global SIFIs can also be subject to additional amounts (‘buffers’) of equity
funding specified by the additional percentage of their risk-weighted assets (RWA).

</details>

#### Short-Answer Questions

1. Briefly describe the liquidity requirements that will apply to the major banks under
Basel III.

> Difficulty: Advanced

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The new liquidity requirements aim to ensure solvent banks remain liquid during periods of
crisis and so are able to honour their obligations to depositors.
The first is the ‘liquidity coverage ratio’ (LCR). This requires banks to hold an amount of high
-quality securities (such as Australian government bonds) to cover the bank’s net cash
outflows for a period of 30 days in case that normal funding markets cease to function. High-
quality securities are expected to remain liquid during these periods.
The second is the ‘net stable funding ratio’ (NSFR) that requires banks to increase their use of
stable funding sources to reduce their funding risks.

</details>

#### Short-Answer Questions

1. Explain the purpose of the capital requirement.

> Difficulty: Intermediate

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The purpose of the capital requirement is to ensure that ADIs have the financial capacity (that is,
equity) to sustain losses, and to encourage their prudent lending. It assigns the proportion of equity an
ADI must hold as a percentage of its risk-weighted assets (RWAs), such as a capital adequacy
requirement of 8 per cent. By using risk-weighted assets, the CAR requires that those assets that have
a greater loss exposure should be supported by more equity than those with a lower loss exposure. The
greater cost associated with equity should also motivate ADIs to curb their appetite for higher-risk
assets.
The way in which the CAR has been implemented has changed over time, starting with the first Basel
Accord in the mid-1980s, revised as Basel II in 2008 and Basel III being fully phased in by 2019.

</details>

2. Briefly discuss the elements of Pillar 1 of the capital requirement.

> Difficulty: Intermediate

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Pillar 1 has three elements
1.The capital requirement: This specifies the minimum amount of funding banks require to have
the capital characteristic of being able to absorb losses.
2.Risk-weighting of assets: The denominator in a bank’s minimum required capital ratio is the
value of its assets adjusted for various forms of risk exposures, where lower risk-taking is
rewarded with discounted asset values. This approach more closely aligns each bank’s capital
requirement with the riskiness of its assets.
3.Minimum leverage ratio: Its purpose is to set a limit on bank leverage.

</details>

### The international financial stability policy

#### Short-Answer Questions

1. Explain how the Financial Stability Board (FSB) sets its financial stability policy agenda.

> Difficulty: Intermediate

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The FSB sets its policy agenda by first identifying the sources of systematic risk to financial
system stability and then determining the policies that can best address these risks. The Board
also monitors the implementation of the policy agenda by member countries. Its effectiveness
relies on cooperation among its member countries, especially those that most influence the
international financial system.
The identification of vulnerabilities in global financial stability is performed by a standing
committee of the FSB’s members, which draws on the research findings and judgements of its
members and those of leading international agencies, such as the IMF, the BIS, the World
Bank, the International Organization of Securities Commissions (IOSCO) and the

### Towards international financial-system

#### Short-Answer Questions

1. Discuss how the Basel Committee on Banking Supervision help maintain consistent and
stronger standard for the prudential supervisions of national banking system.

> Difficulty: Intermediate

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The Basel Committee on Banking Supervision (BCBS) developed guidelines for prudential
supervision to encourage central banks to integrate and harmonise the supervisory
arrangements for their banking system as well as a framework for determining the minimum
capital requirement for their internationally-active banks. The capital requirement framework
was developed to be more sensitive to the risk exposures of individual banks during the early
years of the new century, but its introduction was overtaken by the GFC. The BCBS
responded by further developing its capital requirement framework.

</details>
