### Behavioural finance

#### Short-Answer Questions

1. Explain what is meant by ‘rational expectation’ and how it relates to behavioural finance.

> Difficulty: Intermediate

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Rational expectation refers to the idea that investors’ expectations are formed by objective
analysis free of sentiment. This is the main assumption under the EMH. Contributors to the
field of behavioural finance argue investors display behavioural biases. Examples of
behavioural biases include overconfidence based on ‘self-attribution’ (attributing success to
oneself whether or not this is warranted) and regret aversion, which is a reluctance to exit
poor-performing investments because this would involve an acceptance of losses.

</details>

#### Short-Answer Questions

1. Explain how traders should choose between ‘limit’ orders and ‘at market’ orders

> Difficulty: Advanced

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

A ‘limit’ order specifies a trader’s maximum buy or minimum sell price for a quantity of a security. If
it cannot be satisfied by the orders in the market it will be added to the list of unmet orders (unless it is
cancelled) and may be filled sometime later depending on market price movements.
An ‘at-market’ buy order for a specified quantity of a security will be executed at the best (i.e. lowest)
sell order(s) in the market (from the list of unmet limit orders). Likewise, an ‘at-market’ sell order will
be executed at the best (i.e. highest) buy order(s) in the market.
The main difference between limit orders and market orders relates to the waiting time for
execution and uncertainty regarding execution prices. A trader with short-lived private
information may prefer to exploit this information straight away and hence prefer market
orders over limit orders.

</details>

#### Short-Answer Questions

1. Distinguish between the functions of the primary and secondary markets.

> Difficulty: Intermediate

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The function of the primary market is to raise funds for deficit units through the issue of
securities. Secondary markets do not raise funds for issuers, but they are nevertheless of
fundamental importance to the flow of funds. This is because active secondary markets
provide investors with liquidity – investors would be unwilling to purchase securities in the
primary market if it were not possible to subsequently sell those securities to other investors
in the secondary market when they wish.
Secondary markets also perform price discovery – meaning that the market judges the value
of securities and other financial variables such as interest and exchange rates. This produces
information that is useful to decision making by investors, potential issuers and the wider
community.

</details>

#### Short-Answer Questions

1. Why might a dealer initiate a trade by accepting another dealer’s quote?

> Difficulty: Intermediate

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

#### Short-Answer Questions

1. Briefly outline the three main forms of market efficiency under the EMH.

> Difficulty: Intermediate

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Weak form efficiency says that prices in liquid markets reflect all the information contained in
a security’s past prices. This implies that abnormally profitable trading strategies cannot be
formulated from the study of past prices.
Semi-strong form efficiency assumes that prices in liquid markets also reflect all publically
available information (such as accounting statements and newspaper reports) about the traded
securities and therefore the analysis of this information will not identify abnormally profitable
trading opportunities.
Strong form efficiency is the theory that prices in liquid markets will additionally reflect
unpublished, private information about the traded securities and so access to ‘inside
information’ will not provide trading advantages.

</details>

2. Present arguments that contradict the efficient market hypothesis.

> Difficulty: Intermediate

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Markets do not always behave in a manner that is consistent with market efficiency.
There is evidence of market anomalies whose exploitation can produce excess
returns. These have been observed in firm size, where small firms have

#### Short-Answer Questions

1. Identify the institutions that assist deficit and surplus units to arrange direct financing and
briefly describe their activities.

> Difficulty: Intermediate

2. What are the main costs involved for surplus and deficit units with direct financing?

> Difficulty: Basic

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The cost of funds for deficit units is comprised of the returns they pay to the surplus units who
supply the funds, plus the fees that are paid to the investment bank who assist them to issue
securities. These fees are normally in the form of a commission and will depend on the type of
security being issued. When surplus units supply funds through fund managers they are
charged fees which lower the returns achieved on their investment.

</details>

3. How do the preferences of surplus and deficit units generally differ?

> Difficulty: Basic

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Surplus and deficit units differ in their preferences in flow-of-funds contracts in four ways:
(i)Size mismatch – surplus units normally supply funds in small amounts (through deposits and
superannuation contributions), whereas deficit units usually require large amounts (for example,
for housing and business loans).
(ii)Maturity mismatch – surplus units value liquidity and so prefer short and/or flexible maturities,
whereas deficit units generally desire to use and repay funds over long and/or inflexible terms.
(iii)Risk mismatch – deficit units generally show a greater appetite for risk than surplus units.
(iv)Return mismatch – surplus units prefer to earn higher returns (for a given level of risk),
whereas deficit units prefer to pay a low rate on the funds raised.

</details>

#### Short-Answer Questions

1. Compare the trading arrangements in order-driven and quote-driven markets.

> Difficulty: Intermediate

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The trading arrangements in order-driven markets are determined by the exchange and access
to the market is through brokers. Traders place either limit orders or at-market orders to buy
or sell securities. Brokers act as agents for traders and earn a commission on trades they
facilitate. The trading arrangements were once open-outcry but now use automated trading
systems.
Quote-driven markets are known as ‘over-the-counter’ markets where trading is conducted
either between two dealers or between a dealer and a client. Dealers are principals (they trade
on their own behalf) and act as ‘market-markers’, meaning that they quote bid (buying) and
offer (selling) prices or yields and must trade when a quote is accepted. They earn a spread
(the difference between the prices they buy and sell at) and face price risk on their position
(the inventory of assets they hold).

</details>
