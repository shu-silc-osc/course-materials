#### Short-Answer Questions

1. Suppose an investor holds a 1:4 FRA and interest rates fall unexpectedly. Explain the
value of the FRA to the investor, and how a FRA can be viewed as a derivative.

> Difficulty: Intermediate

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

An investor who plans to buy 90-day bills in one month’s time is faced with the risk that market yields
will fall, causing the price they will have to pay to rise, reducing their returns.
If this investor holds a 1:4 FRA, they will be protected from (hedged against) such a fall in
rates. The FRA will provide the investor with a cash settlement based on the difference
between the agreed forward rate and the spot rate.
The FRA is a derivative because the value to the investor depends on (i.e. is derived from) the
level of the spot rate on the settlement date.

</details>

2. Explain the operation of a 2:5 FRA at 4.50%.

> Difficulty: Basic

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

This is an agreement to settle in two months the difference in the value of the principal
amount should the 90-day spot rate differ from the agreed forward 90-day rate, where a
negative amount represents a payment to the borrower and a positive amount is paid to the
lender.

</details>

3. What is the initial value of an FRA contract and how does it develop value?

> Difficulty: Intermediate

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The initial value of an FRA contract is zero, which is why no payment to the FRA dealer is
required initially. This is because when an FRA is made, it is equally likely that the future spot
rate will be more than or less than the forward rate. Therefore the contract initially has an
expected value of zero.
The contract will develop value, which can be positive or negative, if spot rates move
unexpectedly. If rates increase the contract develops a positive value for borrowers and a
negative value for lenders, and vice versa should interest rates fall. The final value of the
contract is realised on the settlement date as the settlement payment calculated as the
difference between the FRA rate and the current spot rate.

</details>

4. Explain the income of an FRA dealer and how they manage their exposure to interest
rate risk and default risk.

> Difficulty: Intermediate

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

FRA dealers set two-way rates which means the interest rate they agree to with borrowers is 1–2 basis
points higher than what they agree to with lenders. Therefore they earn a spread. The interest rate risk
of FRA dealers is managed by attempting to balance, on a daily basis, the total value of contracts (and
therefore settlement payments) with borrowers and lenders. Should an imbalance develop, the FRA
dealer will hedge their exposure using an FRA or some other type of derivative contract. Dealers are
also exposed to the risk that counterparties fail to pay the settlement payment on the settlement date –
this is default risk. FRA dealers have default exposure limits within which they must remain.

</details>

> Difficulty: Advanced

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

#### Short-Answer Questions

1. Briefly explain the interest rate risk exposures of borrowers and lenders and why
derivative contracts can be can hedge these exposures.

> Difficulty: Basic

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

A floating rate borrower faces the risk of spot interest rates being higher than expected – this
is their ‘downside’ because higher interest rates would increase their financing costs. There is
also the potential ‘upside’ should spot rates be lower than expected. Floating rate lenders have
the opposite exposure, their ‘downside’ is if spot rates are lower than expected because then
they will earn less interest revenue, and the chance of higher rates represents their ‘upside’.
Rather than face the uncertainty of an unknown future spot rate, many borrowers and lenders
will hedge their exposures using derivative contracts. This allows them to lock-in the forward
rate as the effective rate. This is achieved through the derivative contract’s settlement
payment. For example, if the spot rate turns out to be higher than the forward rate, the
derivative contract will produce a positive cash settlement for the borrower which
compensates them for the higher interest paid in the physical market. The higher spot rate
means that lenders will pay the cash settlement, offsetting their higher interest revenue.

</details>

2. Explain why derivative contracts are referred to as ‘risk-transfer’ instruments.

> Difficulty: Intermediate

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

A borrower enters a derivative contract because they do not want to risk having to pay a higher interest
rate than expected, preferring to lock-in the forward rate. However by doing this they give up the
possibility of paying a lower than expected rate. Hence their risk exposure has been changed or
transferred from a risk of higher rates to a risk of missing out on lower rates.
Similarly a lender/investor can transfer their risk of lower than expected returns by entering an
FRA contract but now face the possibility of missing out on higher than expected returns.

</details>

#### Short-Answer Questions

1. Define the term ‘yield curve’ and explain how they are constructed.

> Difficulty: Intermediate

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Yield curves are a graphical representation of the term structure of interest rates. These are the
interest rates that apply to a particular class of assets according to their term. A normal yield
curve is upward sloping and indicates that interest rates are higher as the security’s term
increases. Inverse curves are downward sloping reflecting that short-term rates are higher than
longer-term rates, whereas a flat yield curve shows interest rates are the same regardless of the
security’s term. Yield curves change over time as the economic environment changes.
Yield curves are constructed from interest rates observed in the wholesale financial markets
for a particular class of security, such as BABs. Preferably the security is one with little or no
credit or liquidity risk. It is also important that the security only make one payment at
maturity so their interest rates are for their term to maturity. This is the case with money
market securities, but to construct longer term yield curves, Treasury bonds must be ‘stripped’
of their coupons.

</details>

2. Distinguish between spot and forward rates. What are ‘implied’ forward rates?

> Difficulty: Basic

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Spot interest rates apply to a period of financing that starts today. These are the current
interest rates that are observed in the wholesale financial markets, such as the BBSW.
Forward interest rates apply to a period of financing that begins at a future date. Implicit
forward rates can be calculated from multi-period spot rates, for example if we can observe a
one-period and a two-period spot rate (0r1 and 0r2), from this we can imply the one-period
forward rates that starts in one period (1r2).

</details>

3. Explain how implicit forward rates can be revealed in the money market, and illustrate
your answer with a numerical example.

> Difficulty: Intermediate

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Implicit forward rates can be revealed by comparing the yield for a longer-term security with
that for an equivalent shorter-term security. Since the shorter-term yield forms part of the
longer-term yield, the difference in the yields can be attributed to the yield for the remainder
of the longer term (i.e. the implicit forward rate).
For example, suppose the one-period yield is 5% and the two-period yield is 5.20%. Then the
one-period forward rate is approximately 5.40%.

</details>

> Difficulty: Intermediate

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The price of both bonds will fall if interest rates rise, but the price of longer-termed bonds will
fall by more. This is price risk and it forms the basis for the liquidity premium theory of the
term structure of interest rates.

</details>

2. The liquidity premium theory can explain both the normal and inverse yield curves.

> Difficulty: Intermediate

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

F
The liquidity premium theory argues that long-term bonds have greater price risk than short-
term bonds, and so investors require higher returns on the long-term bonds to compensate
them for the greater risk. The means there is a positive relationship between term and yields,
and results in an upward bias in the yield curve or a normal yield curve. It does not explain an
inverse yield curve.

</details>

#### Short-Answer Questions

1. Explain why there is an inbuilt upward bias in the yield curve according to the liquidity
premium theory of the term structure of interest rates

> Difficulty: Intermediate

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The liquidity premium theory states that longer-term interest rate securities are more risky
than shorter-term interest rate securities. To compensate holders of longer-term interest rate
securities for the additional risk they bear relative to the holders of short-term interest rate
securities, they require higher yields on their investments. This causes an upward bias in the
yield curve i.e. longer-term interest rates have embedded within them a premium not found in
short-term interest rates.
It is important to note, however, that this does not imply that longer-term interest rates will
always be above shorter-term interest rates. Rather, it merely implies that the yield curve is
biased (i.e. has a greater tendency) towards normal.

</details>
