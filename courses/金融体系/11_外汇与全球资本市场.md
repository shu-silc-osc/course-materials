### Explaining exchange rate movements

#### Short-Answer Questions

1. Briefly explain the main theories of exchange rate movements and identify which have been
most relevant to the AUD in recent years.

> Difficulty: Advanced

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Purchasing power parity (PPP) is a long-run theory that argues that higher inflation rates
should lead to currency depreciation based on the ‘law-of-one-price’. Expected interest rate
parity (EIRP) is also a long-run theory that claims that a lower interest rate should be
associated with an expected appreciation of the currency. Another idea is that investors may
lose confidence in the currency of a country with a large accumulated current account deficit.
These theories have not been very useful in explaining recent movements in the AUD.
The following theories are more useful in explaining AUD movements. In contrast to EIRP,
some contend that the expectation of higher interest rates puts upward pressure on the
exchange rate because of investment flows (such as the ‘carry trade’). The terms of trade
concept reasons that when export prices increase relative to import prices, the currency will
appreciate. At times the terms of trade (particularly commodity prices) have had a strong
correlation with the AUD. Another idea is that the exchange rate is sensitive to speculative
flows and there was evidence of this during the GFC. The RBA trade USD/AUD to
counterbalance cyclical price movements that have ‘clearly overshot’ and so are another
influence on the AUD.

</details>

#### Short-Answer Questions

1. Distinguish between ‘spot’ and ‘forward’ foreign exchange contracts.

> Difficulty: Basic

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

All FX contracts specify the amounts of the two currencies that will be exchanged on the
settlement date given the exchange rate at which the contracts were traded. The difference
between spot and forward contracts is when settlement occurs (i.e. when the currencies are
exchanged). Spot contracts are settled in two business days after the trade date, whereas
forward contracts are settled the stated period after the spot settlement date. For example, a
one-month forward contract is settled one month and two business days after the trade date.

</details>

#### Short-Answer Questions

1. What is an FX swap? How can it help an Australian borrower of USD funds avoid FX
risk?

> Difficulty: Intermediate

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

An FX swap is a combination of two FX contracts with different settlement dates that are
agreed simultaneously. The first leg of the swap converts one currency into the desired
currency and the second converts it back to the original currency at a later date. This provides

the use of the desired currency for a defined period.
An Australian borrower of US funds is faced with the risk that the spot price of USD will
increase when they are required to repay the loan. This risk can be avoided through a sell/buy
FX swap. The borrower sells the borrowed USD funds at the spot AUD/USD rate and
simultaneously agrees to buy the repayment amount of USD funds at the forward rate. Hence
they know exactly the cost of the loan in AUD and in USD.

</details>

2. Identify the AUD FX risk exposure of importers, exporters, Australians that lend or
invest offshore and Australians that borrow offshore. How can they manage their FX risk?

> Difficulty: Basic

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Importers will need to purchase foreign exchange in the future and so are exposed to the risk
of a lower AUD, which would increase the cost of the imports. Exporters will receive foreign
exchange in the future which they will sell for AUD. They risk lower export proceeds if the
AUD appreciates.
Australians that lend or invest offshore will want to sell foreign currency for AUD in the
future, and will receive less AUD from these investments if the AUD appreciates. Australians
with foreign debts will have to buy foreign currency in the future to repay loans, and this
becomes more expensive if the AUD depreciates.
These FX risk exposures can be managed with the use of forward contracts because they
establish the exchange rate for a future settlement date, removing the need to transact at the
unknown future spot rate.

</details>
