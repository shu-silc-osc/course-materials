#### Short-Answer Questions

1. Explain how opportunities for comparative advantage may arise.

> Difficulty: Intermediate

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Opportunities for comparative advantage-based strategies rely on the existence of market
segments. These may exist because:
i.Fixed-rate investors could be more sensitive to credit risk than floating-rate
lenders, and thus impose higher risk premiums on borrowers with lower credit
standing.
ii.Borrowers may experience a ‘home-ground advantage’, meaning that they can
borrow on more favourable terms domestically (where they are well known to the
domestic capital market) than is possible in foreign markets.
iii.Taxes and other regulations may impact different groups of borrowers in different
ways, encouraging them to borrow from a particular market despite their interest
rate exposure preferences.

</details>

Credit default swaps (CDSs) are contracts in which the party exposed to credit risk, known as

#### Short-Answer Questions

1. Compare the features of a plain vanilla swap with those of a cross-currency swap.

> Difficulty: Intermediate

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

A plain vanilla swap is an exchange of fixed-for-floating interest payments in the same currency. The
size of these payments depends on the swap rate and BBSW at the commencement of each swap
payment period.
Whereas a cross-currency swap involves the exchange of (i) loan principal amounts in different
currencies at the commencement of the swap, (ii) floating foreign currency interest payments for
floating domestic currency interest payments throughout the swap’s term, and (iii) the return of loan
principal amounts in different currencies at the end of the swap. These swap payments all occur at the
exchange rate when the swap was established.

</details>

2. Explain how an FX swap differs from a cross-currency swap.

> Difficulty: Intermediate

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

An FX swap is a combination of two FX contracts with different settlement dates that are
agreed simultaneously. The first leg of the swap converts one currency into the desired
currency and the second converts it back to the original currency at a later date. This provides
the use of the desired currency for a defined period.
Whereas a cross-currency swap exchanges all the loan payments that occur on a foreign debt – that is,
the principal and its later repayment and a series of floating interest payments. The foreign currency
payments are swapped for domestic currency payments. This manages the foreign exchange risk
associated with a longer-term foreign debt because all the swap payments occur at the exchange rate
set when the swap is established.

</details>

### Fixed-for-floating swaps

#### True/False Questions

1. An interest-rate swap converts a floating-rate borrower into a fixed-rate borrower.

> Difficulty: Intermediate

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

F
The swap does not change the status of the borrowers. It changes the pattern of their payments – so for
example, it can convert a floating-rate borrower into a fixed-rate payer.

</details>

2. In a fixed-for-floating swap, a floating-rate borrower would agree to pay the swap rate,
whereas the fixed-rate borrower would agree to receive the swap rate.

> Difficulty: Intermediate

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

T
By paying the swap rate, the floating-rate borrower becomes a fixed-rate payer, and by
receiving the swap rate, the fixed-rate borrower becomes a floating-rate payer.

</details>

3. BBSW refers to the swap rate in an interest rate swap.

> Difficulty: Basic

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

F
BBSW is the reference rate derived from the bank-bill market each business day that
determines the floating rate at the commencement of each swap period, whereas the swap rate
is the fixed rate at which an individual swap is negotiated.

</details>

4. The payment obligations in a swap are netted at the end of each swap period, and so only a
relatively small cash settlement results.

> Difficulty: Intermediate

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

T
The payments in a swap arise from the difference in the swap and BBSW rates (for either a
quarter or half a year) multiplied by the swap’s nominal amount.

</details>

5. If the BBSW is below the swap rate at the start of the swap period, the fixed-rate payer pays the cash
settlement at the end of the payment period.

> Difficulty: Intermediate

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

T
The fixed and floating interest payments are netted each swap period, and the difference is
payable by the party with the largest swap payment obligation.

</details>

6. Swaps payments are part of each swap party’s debt obligations.

> Difficulty: Intermediate

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

F
Swap is a separate instrument to each party’s debt. Both parties in a swap remain responsible
for their own interest payments and for the repayment of their debt. The swap establishes a
second set of payment obligations, which are settled through swap payments that (along with
their interest payments) are made at the end of each interest (and swap) period.

</details>

#### Short-Answer Questions

1. Why might an ADI that has raised funds in the bond market wish to enter a fixed-for-
floating interest rate swap as the floating-rate payer?

> Difficulty: Intermediate

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

2. How can OIS rates be interpreted as the market’s view regarding the level of cash rates in the

> Difficulty: Intermediate

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The fixed rate in an OIS equates with the expected average cash rate over the term of the swap. The
