#### Short-Answer Questions

1. Define active investment management. How do active managers make investment
decisions?

> Difficulty: Intermediate

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Active investment managers attempt to make superior investment selection and timing
decisions with the aim of achieving above average returns. This means that poorly performing
assets within portfolios will be sold and replaced with assets the manager expects to do better.
Clearly these managers are not advocates of the efficient market hypothesis.
Managers will attempt to identify underpriced or overpriced assets through technical analysis
and/or fundamental analysis. Technical analysis is also known as charting. It is the analysis of
past prices in the attempt to identify future price movements (trends or patterns). Their
approaches include momentum and contrarian investing. Fundamental analysis is the analysis
of the factors that determine an asset’s future cash flows to identify its fair (or true) value.
Techniques include discounted cash flow analysis and financial statement analysis.

</details>

2. What is the main similarity between momentum and contrarian investment strategies?
What is the main difference between them?

> Difficulty: Advanced

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The main similarity between the two strategies is that they are both sentiment based and their
success hinges upon the investor being able to accurately predict sentiment.
The main difference between the two strategies is that while a momentum strategy involves
buying in a rising market and selling in a falling market, a contrarian strategy involves buying
in a falling market and selling in a rising market. This is because momentum traders believe
prices take time to move to their new fair values after the release of new information, whereas
contrarian traders believe that prices overshoot because the market overacts to this same
information. Neither strategy is consistent with the EMH.

</details>

#### Short-Answer Questions

1. What are hedge funds? Distinguish between US and Australian hedge funds.

> Difficulty: Intermediate

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Hedge funds are a form of collective investment that uses a variety of complex and non-
traditional investment strategies and very high levels of leverage in an attempt to earn high
returns for investors including during periods when share prices are falling. The strategies
used by them include short selling, arbitrage, derivatives trading and program trading.
The US hedge fund industry includes very large, unregulated funds that take an aggressive
approach to investing and expose their investors to large risks. In Australia the hedge fund
industry is relatively small and funds are subject to ASIC regulations that require them to
disclose their investment plans and fees.

</details>

2. What is the main advantage and disadvantage of funds of hedge funds (FOHFs)?

> Difficulty: Intermediate

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

In Australia about one-third of hedge fund investments are held by funds of hedge funds.
Individual hedge funds may undertake risky investment strategies in the pursuit of high
returns irrespective of the investment environment and thus pose undiversified risk. FOHFs
serve to diversify risk and so can achieve a target rate of return with lower risk.
The fees of hedge fund managers can substantially erode the returns to the investors and
FOHFs involve double-up on fees; those of the individual fund managers and those of the
FOHF manager.

</details>

#### Short-Answer Questions

1. Describe the process of funds management.

> Difficulty: Intermediate

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Fund managers are financial institutions that attract retail investment funds and ‘pool’ them
for wholesale market investments in a particular type or mix of assets. They can offer
investors a wide range of products with different levels of risk and return, and earn fees for
the provision of these investment services. Note that:
(i)fund managers do not bear the investment risks – rather these are borne by investors; and
(ii)a retail fund manager would normally attract the funds and then allocate them to a
wholesale manager who would then be responsible for investing the funds in assets.

</details>

2. Identify and briefly describe the main groups of fund managers.

> Difficulty: Basic

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The three main groups of fund managers are superannuation, insurance and public unit trusts.
Superannuation is by far the largest category in Australia. Superannuation funds collect
contributions made during a person’s working life and invest those funds for the purpose of
generating income in retirement.
Insurance companies (particularly life insurance) collect premiums some of which are
invested to form a pool of assets from which claims are funded.
Public unit trusts provide retail investors an opportunity to invest in a range of asset class
portfolios by purchasing units in the trust.

</details>

3. What type of assets do fund managers invest in?

> Difficulty: Basic

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Fund managers invest in a variety of asset classes. The largest share of managed-fund assets
are invested in shares and units in listed trusts. The other main classes are bonds and money
market securities, cash and deposits, investments in overseas assets, property, and alternative
investments such as infrastructure, hedge funds and private equity funds.

</details>

#### Short-Answer Questions

1. Explain the approach taken by passive investment managers.

> Difficulty: Basic

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Passive managers construct portfolios that achieve returns that closely match those of a
benchmark index, such as the ASX200. They do not attempt to make superior stock selection
and timing decisions but rather hold securities in proportion to their weight within the
benchmark index. These holdings would change only when the composition of the index is
changed.
Their rationale is that over long periods, investment managers are unlikely to outperform the
benchmark index (if the market is efficient) and so they should aim to lower their transaction
costs.

</details>

In a listed unit trust, units in the trust are listed on an exchange (such as the ASX) and thus
investors can enter and exit investments via market transactions. The main advantage of listed
funds is that the secondary market provides liquidity. These trusts are usually closed-ended,
which means that transactions between investors do not change the number of units in the
fund.
Unlisted unit trusts are generally open-ended, so as funds flow into and out of the fund the
number of units will change. Entry and exit values are determined by the trust (based on
valuations of its assets) rather than as assessed by the market.

</details>

#### Short-Answer Questions

1. Distinguish between defined benefit and accumulation superannuation schemes. Clearly
explain which party bears the investment risk in each arrangement.

> Difficulty: Advanced

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

An accumulation scheme is one in which the benefits to the contributor at retirement are
uncertain because they depend upon the returns achieved by investments. Clearly this
arrangement exposes the contributor to investment risk. To illustrate, an individual who
contributes $200 per month for 35 years will accumulate $1 286 191.89 if average returns are
12 per cent per annum compounding monthly, but only $284 942.06 if average returns are 6
per cent per annum. Most superannuation assets are in accumulation schemes.
A defined benefit scheme pays the contributor a specified amount at retirement, either as a lump sum
or as an annuity – for example, an income equal to 66 per cent of the contributor’s pre-retirement
income for as long as they live. This exposes the scheme (or employer) to the risk that contributions by
the employee will accumulate to an insufficient amount to pay the agreed benefits, requiring the
scheme (or employer) to make up the difference.

</details>

2. Explain the three main factors that will determine the accumulated sum at retirement in an
accumulation scheme.

> Difficulty: Basic

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

(i)The size of the regular contributions, which would normally increase over a person’s working life.
(ii)The impact of compounding, given that funds cannot be withdrawn prior to retirement.
(iii)The returns achieved on the investment of the funds (net of fees paid).

</details>

3. Distinguish between ‘balanced’ and ‘growth’ superannuation portfolios.

> Difficulty: Basic

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Most superannuation schemes offer their contributors a choice of investment portfolios.
‘Growth’ portfolios comprise a greater proportion of growth assets (shares and property) and

exact asset mix will differ between schemes.

2. Say your funds were invested with an active fund manager who performed relatively

manager?

> Difficulty: Advanced

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The main problem is the variable persistence in the performance of active fund managers. The
evidence suggests that fund performance is unstable and that past performance is not a good
predictor of future performance. This means that it is difficult to judge: (i) whether your
current manager’s poor performance will continue; and (ii) whether an alternative manager’s
better performance can be maintained. Ratings provided by Morningstar and S&P would
provide you with more information about your current investment fund and it may be worth
considering moving from a one- or two-star rated fund to a four- or five-star rated fund.
However funds charge entry and exit fees (as well as ongoing management fees) so the impact
of these fees also needs to be considered before switching.

</details>

2. Explain the role of the trustee in managed superannuation schemes.

> Difficulty: Intermediate

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Registered superannuation funds (that is all funds except SMSF) have a trustee. The trustee holds the
fund’s assets in trust on behalf of its members and it is their responsibility to represent the fund’s
members. The trustee reviews the investment decisions made by the fund’s managers and ensures
these are consistent with the fund’s trust deed and the desire to maximise returns with regard to the
risks involved. Trustees have a fiduciary duty of care over the operations of the fund, meaning that
they must carry out their duties with a high level of regard for the risks involved.

</details>
