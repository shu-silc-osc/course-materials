> Difficulty: Basic

### Long-term financial markets

#### Short-Answer Questions

1. Explain how residential mortgage-backed securities (RMBS) allow investor’s funds to
be used to finance housing loans.

> Difficulty: Intermediate

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

In RMBS banks can sell some of their existing housing loans to a special-purpose vehicle
(SPV). The SPV arranges the sale of the MBS to investors and uses the proceeds to pay the
bank for the loans. This process is called securitisation. Repayments by borrowers flow
through to investors in the securities, less fees deducted by the loan seller and SPV. In this
way, the housing loans are funded directly by the investors in the MBS.

</details>

### Prudent lending standard

#### Short-Answer Questions

1. Australian ADIs have generally applied conservative lending standards when making
housing loans. Describe the four main elements of these standards. Describe two ways in
which these standards were not applied in the US sub-prime loan market prior to the GFC.

> Difficulty: Intermediate

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Prudent lending requires that loans be secured by a mortgage over the property, that the loans
do not usually exceed 80 per cent of the property’s value, that borrowers are assessed as
having the capacity to make repayments and that the lender has reliable documentary
evidence of both the property’s value and the borrower’s capacity to repay.
Often borrowers in US sub-prime loans could not have been assessed as having the capacity
to repay loans, because they were unemployed or had a history of loan defaults, but were
granted loans nonetheless. In addition, loan amounts often exceeded 80 per cent of the
property value and loans for 100 per cent were not uncommon.

</details>

#### Short-Answer Questions

1. Briefly describe the sources of funds for banks and their relative importance in terms of
the amounts raised.

> Difficulty: Intermediate

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Banks raise funds from domestic retail deposits, short-term securities (issued domestically or
overseas), bonds (issued domestically or overseas), equity and securitisation.
Since the GFC the banking system has increased its reliance on deposits, and these supply (as of 2013)
about 55 per cent of banks’ funding. The reliance on the financial markets has fallen to about 35 per
cent (as of 2013) of banks’ funding with similar amounts being raised in long- and short-term markets,
whereas previously the short-term markets were a substantially greater source of funds. Most of the
remaining funds are supplied by equity with securitisation just providing approximately 1 to 2 per
cent.

</details>

2. Briefly describe the main debt securities issued by banks in the financial markets.

> Difficulty: Intermediate

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Australian banks borrow funds from both the domestic and overseas financial markets.
In the domestic markets banks borrow more from the short-term money market, although this
has fallen substantially since the GFC. The principal instrument is a negotiable certificate of
deposit (or NCD) which represent a promise by the bank to repay a wholesale deposit with
interest on the agreed maturity date. Being negotiable means NCDs can be traded in the
money market and this feature gives the deposits liquidity. The domestic bond market is
mainly used by the major banks who issue unsecured bonds with a wide range of maturities,
although three to five years is most common.
Australian banks raise more funds in overseas markets than they do domestically. This is
mostly in the form of bonds, either as unsecured medium-term bonds or long-term covered
bonds. The amounts raised are normally in foreign currency which the bank then swaps into
Australian dollars. Covered bonds are secured by high-quality bank assets which improve
their credit rating (to AAA) and so reduces the interest cost to the bank.

</details>

3. Explain a typical ADI’s sources and uses of funds.

> Difficulty: Intermediate

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

An ADI raises debt funds from deposits and through the issue of securities in the financial
markets. ADIs offer a range of retail deposit accounts that can be classified as at-call, savings
or term deposits. They issue securities in the domestic money and bond markets, however
more frequently they will use offshore markets. The domestic issues are mostly negotiable
certificates of deposit and unsecured, medium-term bonds. The offshore issues are also
unsecured, medium-term bonds and also long-term covered bonds. Equity is also a source of
funds.
ADIs have two main uses of funds. The most important (as a proportion of their assets) is
their investment in loans and advances, of which housing loans constitute the largest category.
An ADI will also invest in cash and securities. Securities are held for liquidity purposes, to
earn a return and to trade. Bill acceptances are another significant use of funds by some ADIs.

</details>

4. Explain how banks set their interest rates on loans.

> Difficulty: Intermediate

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Interest rates are set to cover the bank’s cost of funds, its other operating costs, provisions for
loan losses and its profit margin. The rate set for housing loans will apply to all such loans.
For business loans these factors determine the prime interest rate (which is the rate payable on
loans to their lowest risk business borrowers) to which an additional margin is added
dependent on the risk of the particular borrower.
Banks also need to ensure their interest rates are competitive with those offered by other
institutions, without threatening their capacity to make adequate provisions for bad debts.

</details>
